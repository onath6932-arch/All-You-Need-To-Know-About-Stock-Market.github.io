Preface & Introduction

Framework for Rational Decision Making

Investing does not require superior intelligence, insider knowledge, or complex math. It requires a sound intellectual framework to make decisions and emotional discipline to keep feelings from ruining that framework.

"To invest successfully over a lifetime does not require a stratospheric IQ... What's needed is a sound intellectual framework." — Warren E. Buffett

Two Key Lessons

  • Growth ≠ Profit: High corporate growth prospects rarely translate into automatic investor returns due to overpaying for hype.
  • Expert Fallibility: Wall Street professionals do not have reliable ways to consistently predict market trends or stock performance.
Chapter 1

Investment vs. Speculation

Graham defines an investment operation rigorously:

Graham's Investment Triad
1. Thorough financial analysis of business fundamentals.
2. Safety of principal protection against severe losses.
3. Adequate return expectation (rather than getting rich quick).

Operations failing any of these three requirements are speculative. Unintelligent speculation occurs when speculating under the illusion that you are investing.

Chapter 2

Inflation & Asset Classes

Equities are not a perfect hedge against inflation, but they offer far better long-term purchasing power protection than fixed-income assets (bonds and cash).

Key Takeaways

  • Inflation erodes fixed yields over time.
  • Corporate earnings do not automatically scale upward during high inflation due to rising capital costs.
  • REITs and TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) provide supplemental safety.
Chapter 3

A Century of Stock Market History

Historical market cycles show that extreme bull markets always end in corrections. Investors must study market history to avoid projecting recent price gains indefinitely into the future.

Chapter 4

Asset Allocation Strategy

Graham proposes a rules-based allocation for defensive investors to keep emotion out of market timing:

The 25/75 Allocation Rule
• Maintain a baseline split of 50% Common Stocks / 50% High-Grade Bonds.
• Shift up to 75% stocks when markets are abnormally cheap.
• Drop down to 25% stocks when markets reach extreme valuation highs.
Chapter 5

The Defensive Investor & Common Stocks

Defensive investors prioritize safety and freedom from stress over market-beating returns. They achieve this via broad diversification across leading, financially sound companies or index funds.

Chapter 6

The Enterprising Investor Strategy: Negative Approach

Enterprising (active) investors should start by avoiding high-risk, low-probability plays:

  • Avoid junk bonds and high-yield preferred stocks without sufficient coverage.
  • Avoid foreign bonds unless priced at severe panic discounts.
  • Avoid IPOs (Initial Public Offerings), which are built to favor sellers over buyers.
Chapter 7

Second-Discipline Stock Selection

To achieve superior results, the active investor should focus on:

  • Buying in depressed markets: Capitalizing on overall panic.
  • Bargain Issues (Net-Nets): Buying stocks selling below their Net Current Asset Value (NCAV).
  • Special Situations: Arbitrage, liquidations, and corporate reorganizations.
Chapter 8

Market Fluctuations & "Mr. Market"

Graham introduces his famous allegory: Imagine buying a share of a business where your partner is an emotional character named Mr. Market.

"Mr. Market is your servant, not your guide. He offers prices every day based on his mood swings—ranging from wild euphoria to deep depression. You are free to ignore him or exploit his folly."

Do not mistake market noise for business value updates.

Chapter 9

Investing in Investment Funds

Most active mutual funds fail to beat market indexes over long horizons due to high management fees, trading costs, and cash drag. Indexing is recommended for most individual investors.

Chapter 10

Advisors & Money Managers

Be cautious with financial advisers. Seek independent advisers who focus on capital preservation rather than those selling commission-based financial products.

Chapter 11

Security Analysis for Laymen

Focus analysis on bond coverage, earnings stability, balance sheet strength, dividend track records, and realistic growth forecasts.

Chapter 12

Per-Share Earnings Analysis

Don't take single-year Earnings Per Share (EPS) at face value. Look at 7-to-10-year average earnings to account for accounting tricks, dilution, and economic cycles.

Chapter 13

Comparing Four Listed Companies

Graham works through direct comparisons of balance sheets and income statements, proving that popular growth stocks are often priced for perfection, creating asymmetric downside risk.

Chapter 14

Stock Selection for Defensive Investors

7 Defensive Selection Rules
1. Adequate Size: Minimum revenue threshold to rule out small, fragile firms.
2. Strong Finances: Current assets at least 2x current liabilities; debt < Net Current Assets.
3. Earnings Stability: Positive earnings in each of the past 10 years.
4. Dividend Record: Uninterrupted dividends for at least 20 years.
5. Earnings Growth: Min 33% growth in EPS over the past decade.
6. Moderate P/E Ratio: Price no more than 15x average 3-year earnings.
7. Moderate P/B Ratio: Price to Book < 1.5 (or P/E x P/B < 22.5).
Chapter 15

Stock Selection for Enterprising Investors

Enterprising investors seek larger returns by applying looser financial size rules while insisting on lower valuation metrics (e.g., buying below 120% of net tangible assets or using Net Current Asset Value formulas).

Chapter 16

Convertible Issues & Warrants

Convertible bonds and warrants often promise the benefits of both stocks and bonds, but in practice, they are often structured to favor issuers rather than buyers.

Chapter 17

Four Instructive Case Histories

Case studies showing how financial engineering, excessive leverage, and speculative market manias destroy long-term investor capital.

Chapter 18

Comparison of Eight Pairs of Companies

Side-by-side comparison illustrating how market prices regularly misvalue companies with identical underlying fundamental earnings power.

Chapter 19

Stockholders & Management Policy

Shareholders are owners. They should actively vote against ineffective management teams, demand rational dividend policies, and ensure capital isn't wasted on poor acquisitions.
